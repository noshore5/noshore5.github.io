Four models, one shared blind spot: a full GRU / Mamba / DBConformer / SlimSeiz comparison

2026-08-25 · architecture comparison, seizure prediction

With GRU, Mamba, DBConformer, and SlimSeiz each independently tuned to their own best-known configuration under the same six-fold, chb01, leave-one-seizure-out protocol, this note lines all four up side by side for the first time. The headline aggregate numbers tell one story. Looking at them fold-by-fold tells a very different one — and overturns which model actually "wins."

Caveat up front: this isn't a controlled experiment. GRU and Mamba ran on Windows/CUDA and consume this repo's own CWT+dense-edge-graph features; DBConformer and SlimSeiz ran on a Mac (MPS) and classify raw EEG windows directly. Four different runs, four different seeds, no error bars — single-run point estimates, each pipeline's own best-known config rather than a symmetric sweep applied evenly across all four.

Read at face value: Mamba wins on average precision, the metric treated as primary throughout this project. GRU wins recall and f1. SlimSeiz is the quietest alarm stream but misses the most seizures. One fold breaks the aggregate story

Average precision is threshold-independent; recall and f1 aren't. Breaking those two down by fold shows something the aggregate table hides completely — a single seizure, 1_18_0, where every raw-EEG model's recall collapses to almost nothing, while GRU aces it:

1_18_0 is GRU's single best fold on both recall and f1, and it's every other pipeline's worst or tied-worst fold. That's not a subtle effect on the aggregate — it's most of the gap that made GRU look like the recall/f1 winner in the first place. Dropping that one fold and recomputing the mean over the other five inverts the ranking:

Same flip on recall specifically for Mamba, the pipeline whose 6-fold recall trailed GRU's by the widest margin: exclude 1_18_0 and GRU barely moves (0.807→0.814, it was already fine on the other five), while Mamba jumps from 0.750 to 0.880 — a lead that flips to Mamba by 0.066.

The "GRU has the best recall/f1" finding is substantially an artifact of one fold, not a consistent five-of-six advantage. Drop 1_18_0 and GRU goes from best f1 to worst of the four, while Mamba, DBConformer, and SlimSeiz all pull ahead of it. It cuts the other way from the story you'd tell about Mamba being "dragged down by one bad fold": Mamba's f1 was pulled down by 1_18_0 in absolute terms, but so was DBConformer's and SlimSeiz's, by similar amounts. GRU is the actual outlier here — propped up by a fold none of the raw-EEG models can get any recall on at all.

The AP table tells a subtler version of the same story. On AP specifically, 1_18_0 isn't uniformly catastrophic — Mamba scores 0.618 there, its second-best fold, and DBConformer's 0.362 is genuinely its worst. So the models aren't failing to rank preictal windows above interictal ones on this fold; they're failing at the fixed 0.5 decision threshold specifically. That points at a calibration or threshold problem on this one fold rather than the raw-EEG representations having no signal there at all — worth checking directly, and flagged now in three separate session notes without being investigated.

Reading each model on its own terms

Mamba wins on AP (0.499, next-best DBConformer at 0.442) and ties for the best smoothed hit-rate reliability, at the best raw FAR/hr among the two dense-edge models. It's the strongest single number here on the metric this project treats as primary, at roughly 4.6× GRU's per-epoch wall-clock cost on this hardware (a pure-PyTorch scan, no fused kernel) — real, but not something that changes which model performs best.

GRU has the best raw recall/f1 headline numbers but the worst AP and highest FAR/hr of the four — it over-predicts positive more than the others, a pattern also flagged (more mildly) for DBConformer. As shown above, its f1 lead is mostly one fold; excluding it, GRU has the worst f1 of the four.

DBConformer never leads and never trails on any metric — consistent with being an unmodified architecture adapted to this protocol rather than purpose-built or tuned for it.

SlimSeiz is a different operating point rather than uniformly worse: best smoothed FAR/hr and best raw accuracy, but the lowest recall and the only pipeline to miss two of six seizures under smoothing instead of one. Its AP (0.431) is inflated somewhat by one outlier fold — 1_16_0 scores a perfect AP of 1.000, its easiest fold by a wide margin across all four pipelines — while 1_15_0 and 1_18_0 are among its weakest. The mean does some work papering over a wide per-fold spread.

1_26_0 is worth a brief mention too: every pipeline's second- or third-lowest AP, and GRU's only smoothed miss here — but not a shared miss across pipelines the way 1_18_0 is. Just a hard fold for everyone, in absolute terms.

Open items

What's actually different about 1_18_0's preictal windows — in whichever feature space each model sees them — that only GRU's dense-edge CWT+graph representation handles at the default decision threshold, remains unexplained after three notes flagging it. Nothing here has error bars: every number is a single run at each pipeline's own best-known config, not a hyperparameter search applied evenly across all four, so none of this should be read as a hard ceiling for any architecture. And SlimSeiz's number here is its adaptive-channel-selection variant specifically (the one with complete data) — its fixed-montage variant tied it on most metrics and is operationally safer, but isn't the headline only because part of its result set was lost to an unrelated crash.
